\begin{abstract}
\textbf{[TODO --- STUB]:} Draft abstract. Core claim: the same interventional Shapley-attribution
mechanism (Budhathoki et al., ICML 2022, run forward on $do(x)$ samples
instead of backward on observed outliers) that a companion systems paper
argues gives causal capacity forecasting an explanatory advantage over
black-box regression is only faithful along one of its two claimed axes.
Across a battery of controlled experiments on two microservice DAGs of
different scale (Sock Shop, 28 fitted nodes; Train Ticket, 112), the
mechanism is a reliable, zero-false-positive detector of \emph{local}
(Tier-2) resource anomalies but an unreliable attributor of
\emph{propagated} (Tier-1) network-driven ones -- accuracy ranging
$0$--$80\%$ across individual Train Ticket nodes at one causal hop from the
injection point, recovering to $90$--$92.5\%$ at two-to-three hops once
credit correctly flowing through validated backpressure (cross-service
$R{\to}R$) edges is counted as ``upstream'' rather than silently dropped.
We do not stop at reporting this asymmetry: three follow-up diagnostics
trace it to a specific, mechanistic cause rather than leaving it as an
unexplained scale effect -- per-node NNLS coefficient inspection shows the
target's own fitted mechanism assigns only $1.5$--$10.8\%$ of explained
variance to direct workload propagation regardless of node, and a direct
measurement of the true propagated signal (how far a $do(x)$ intervention
actually moves each target's own workload node, in units of that node's own
training standard deviation) shows this varies by a factor of two across
nodes at identical hop-distance, explaining part -- but not all -- of which
specific nodes fail. A scoped comparison against BARO (Nguyen et al.,
FSE'24), a non-causal statistical RCA baseline, on the two most diagnostic
nodes shows neither method dominates: BARO outperforms Shapley on the node
where Shapley fails worst, and underperforms it on the node where Shapley
succeeds -- evidence that attributing network-propagated causes correctly
is a task both approaches find genuinely hard at this scale, not a defect
unique to interventional Shapley. We report every negative result,
including two of our own analysis bugs found and corrected mid-study
(a backpressure-edge misclassification, and a parent-column ordering error),
with a ground-truth-by-construction methodology throughout.
\end{abstract}

\section{Results: Four Faithfulness Experiments}
\label{sec:results}

Section~\ref{sec:background} argues that a projected $do(x)$ outcome can be \emph{decomposed} -- attributed to specific downstream services and mechanisms -- via the same Shapley-value machinery Budhathoki et al.\ \cite{budhathoki2022rootcause} use for outlier root-cause attribution, run forward instead of backward. This capability is implemented (\texttt{gcm.attribute\_anomalies} on interventional samples, in a module separate from the production capacity-forecasting agent evaluated in the companion systems paper) but had not been checked for basic trustworthiness before this study. We run four validity checks -- three on Sock Shop's 28-node DAG (one shared with Train Ticket), one on Train Ticket's larger graph -- none of which requires an LLM API, so all four are cheap to reproduce and re-run.

\subsection{Null-Condition False-Positive Rate}
\label{sec:null-fp}
We run $do(\text{front-end\_workload} = \bar{w}_{\text{train}})$ -- i.e.\ no real change -- 20 independent times (Monte Carlo draws differ only by seed) and check how often the existing risk-level heuristic (a fixed percentage-change threshold, or an IT-score threshold from \texttt{gcm.anomaly\_scores}) flags a node ``warning'' or ``critical'' anyway. \textbf{This uncovered a real calibration bug}: at $85\%$ scenario-level (i.e.\ at least one false alarm in 17/20 runs), concentrated almost entirely in one node (\texttt{catalogue\_cpu}, flagged in 85\% of runs). The cause is a small-denominator artifact: \texttt{catalogue\_cpu}'s baseline value is small (${\sim}0.23$ in its native units), so a purely Monte Carlo absolute deviation reads as a percentage swing as large as $+118\%$ under a condition with no real change at all. We fixed this by requiring every risk-level trigger -- both the percentage-change path and the IT-score path -- to also clear a z-score gate ($|{\text{predicted}-\text{baseline}}| / \sigma_{\text{train}} \geq 1$ for warning, $\geq 2$ for critical), computed against each node's own training-period noise rather than a fixed cross-node number. A first fix that gated only the percentage path reduced the false-positive rate to $65\%$; gating the IT-score path as well eliminated it, at $0.0\%$ across all 20 repeats. We report both the bug and the two-step fix rather than only the final number, since the intermediate result shows the bug was not a single trivial typo.

\subsection{Dose-Response Monotonicity}
\label{sec:dose-response}
For the node most directly downstream of the injection point (\texttt{front-end\_cpu}), we ran 5 independent repeats at each of 5 intervention levels ($0\%$ to $+300\%$) and computed the Spearman correlation between intervention magnitude and (i) the raw IT-based anomaly score and (ii) the Shapley contribution, across three independent executions of this exact experiment (before and after the calibration fix in Section~\ref{sec:null-fp}, which does not itself change either quantity -- only which repeats it retroactively would have flagged). \textbf{The two diverge sharply.} Shapley contribution's correlation with intervention magnitude was stable and strong across all three runs ($0.90$, $1.00$, $1.00$) and, in the post-fix run, translates into a sensible decision boundary: $0$--$20\%$ load stays \textsc{normal}, $50\%$ consistently triggers \textsc{warning} ($z\approx2.0$), and $150$--$300\%$ consistently triggers \textsc{critical} ($z\approx6$--$12$), with zero false alarms at $0\%$ across 5 repeats. The raw anomaly score's correlation, by contrast, was unstable and at one point \emph{negative} across the same three runs ($-0.50$, $0.50$, $0.60$) -- it is not reliably monotonic run-to-run. \textbf{We flag this explicitly}: the Shapley-based attribution this paper evaluates behaves like a trustworthy signal under this check, but the raw per-node anomaly score it is partly built from does not, and should not be read as validated by this result.

\subsection{Topology Respect (Train Ticket)}
\label{sec:topology}
Sock Shop's 7-service graph is too small and too densely reachable from its single gateway for a clean negative-control test -- nearly every node is downstream of \texttt{front-end}. Train Ticket's fitted accurate-path DAG is not: from the natural gateway \texttt{ts-preserve-service}, 17 of the 27 other services are reachable (hops 1--3) and \textbf{10 are genuinely unreachable} (no path exists in the fitted graph at all), giving a principled test -- attribution should not meaningfully favor unreachable services over reachable ones. We intervened at $+150\%$ for 10 independent repeats and ranked all service CPU nodes by Shapley contribution each time.

\textbf{Current result (re-run against the DAG's present state, which now includes the validated backpressure edges of Section~\ref{sec:tier-v2}): Precision@17 $= 75.9\%$}, against a $63.0\%$ chance baseline -- directionally correct but not a strong margin. Grouped by topological role: mean Shapley contribution is $0.161$ (injection node's own CPU), $0.242$ (reachable services), $0.191$ (unreachable services) -- reachable services do score higher than unreachable ones on average, though the gap is modest relative to the per-service spread, and the injection node's own CPU is, perhaps counter-intuitively, not the highest-scoring group.

An earlier pass of this same check (recorded in this document before the backpressure-edge extension of Section~\ref{sec:tier-v2} was added to both testbeds) had found Precision@17 $=60.0\%$ -- \emph{below} chance -- and, after ruling out an inconsistently-applied monotonicity constraint as the cause (re-fitting both DAGs identically moved the number only to $60.6\%$), reported this as an open, unexplained negative result. \textbf{We flag rather than quietly overwrite the discrepancy}: we do not have direct evidence disentangling whether the shift from $60.0\%$ to $75.9\%$ reflects the backpressure edges changing the fitted DAG, or ordinary Monte Carlo variance between two independent 10-repeat runs (a sample size too small to rule out $\pm15$ points of run-to-run noise on its own). What both runs agree on is the qualitative conclusion: Shapley's topology-respecting signal at Train Ticket's scale is real but weak, sitting close enough to chance that it should not be reported as a strong validation on its own -- consistent with the more diagnostic, per-node findings of Sections~\ref{sec:tier-v2}--\ref{sec:propagation}.

\subsection{Tier-1 vs.\ Tier-2 Decomposition}
\label{sec:tier}
Section~\ref{sec:background} makes a more specific claim than any check above tests: that Shapley attribution can decompose a projected change at \emph{one} target node into how much comes from Tier-1 network propagation versus Tier-2 local resource conversion. Neither the dose-response check (Section~\ref{sec:dose-response}; its target, the injection node itself, has no Tier-1 ancestor to separate from) nor the topology check (Section~\ref{sec:topology}; which compares totals \emph{across} services, not tiers \emph{within} one) actually exercises this. We test it directly with a ground-truth-by-construction design: for each of several downstream targets, we construct a \emph{Tier-1-driven} anomaly (a real $do(\text{gateway\_workload} = 2.5\times)$ intervention, target's own value left to the fitted mechanism) and a \emph{Tier-2-driven} anomaly (no real workload change, but the target's own resource value manually perturbed by $+5\sigma$ -- simulating a local inefficiency independent of traffic). We check whether the tier with the larger $|{\text{contribution}}|$ matches the known true cause, over 10 independent repeats $\times$ 6 Sock Shop targets and 10 repeats $\times$ 4 Train Ticket targets.

\textbf{Current result: the asymmetry replicates, and Tier-2 remains essentially perfect} -- $100.0\%$ accuracy on both systems, 120/120 and 80/80 trials, unchanged from the first pass of this check. Tier-1 (propagation) accuracy is $78.3\%$ on Sock Shop (47/60) and $17.5\%$ on Train Ticket (7/40). (An earlier recorded pass of this exact check had found $60.0\%$ on Sock Shop; the Train Ticket figure is unchanged. As in Section~\ref{sec:topology}, we do not have a fully disentangled explanation for the Sock Shop shift -- the underlying DAG-fitting code for both testbeds was modified between recordings to add the backpressure edges of Section~\ref{sec:tier-v2} -- and report the current number as authoritative rather than silently reconcile it.) \textbf{We revise the claim accordingly}: attributing a resource anomaly to the target's own local mechanism is a validated capability; attributing it to upstream network propagation is not reliable, and is markedly worse at Train Ticket's scale. The remainder of this section (Sections~\ref{sec:tier-v2}--\ref{sec:baro}) goes beyond the original four-experiment scope to explain \emph{why}.

\subsection{A Classification Bug and Its Correction}
\label{sec:tier-v2}
The result above uses the same two-way classifier as the original check: every contributor returned by the attribution call is bucketed as Tier~1 if its node name ends in \texttt{\_workload}, or as Tier~2 if it is the target's own key. This classifier predates a change to both testbeds' DAG-construction code that added \emph{validated backpressure edges} -- a second, cross-service parent added to specific CPU nodes (a directly-calling service's own CPU, capturing synchronous backpressure a callee's request count alone does not; validated against held-out data in the companion systems paper's own accuracy checks before deployment). A backpressure parent's node name ends in \texttt{\_cpu}, not \texttt{\_workload} and not the target's own key -- so the two-way classifier \textbf{silently drops it from both tier sums}. Cross-referencing the backpressure-edge list against this section's test targets shows the omission is not a corner case: \textbf{3 of 6 Sock Shop targets} (\texttt{shipping\_cpu}, \texttt{carts\_cpu}, \texttt{user\_cpu}) and \textbf{4 of 4 Train Ticket targets} (\texttt{ts-order/user/travel/seat-service\_cpu}) have at least one backpressure parent whose contribution the original classifier never counted.

We built a corrected, three-way classifier -- Tier~1 (\texttt{*\_workload}), backpressure (any other \texttt{*\_cpu}/\texttt{*\_mem}/\texttt{*\_latency-50} node), Tier~2 (the target's own key) -- and re-ran the identical protocol, reporting two accuracy numbers per trial: \emph{strict} (does Tier~1 alone dominate Tier~2 alone, the original standard) and \emph{upstream} (does Tier~1$+$backpressure combined dominate Tier~2 -- the practically relevant question for a bottleneck-ranking heuristic, and the fairer reading given the companion paper's own Section on backpressure edges already flags that they narrow the tier-decomposition's clean two-way interpretability on exactly these targets). \textbf{The correction does not rescue the Train Ticket result}: strict accuracy is $73.3\%$ (Sock Shop) and $15.0\%$ (Train Ticket); upstream accuracy is $73.3\%$ (Sock Shop, unchanged -- none of its three affected targets flip) and $32.5\%$ (Train Ticket, roughly double the strict figure but still worse than a coin flip). \textbf{We conclude the classification bug is not the explanation for Train Ticket's weakness} -- it is a genuine property of the fitted mechanism at this scale, which the next two subsections trace to a specific cause.

\subsection{Does Attribution Accuracy Track Hop-Distance?}
\label{sec:hop-distance}
Section~\ref{sec:tier-v2}'s four Train Ticket targets were, by coincidence of which nodes had a convenient ground-truth-by-construction setup, all exactly one hop from the injection gateway -- so the data could not distinguish ``attribution is unreliable in general'' from ``attribution is unreliable specifically at short range, and the four targets tested happened to sample only that range.'' We extended the identical tier1\_driven/tier2\_driven protocol to five additional Train Ticket targets spanning hops 2 (\texttt{ts-config/basic/train/route-service\_cpu}) and hop 3 (\texttt{ts-price-service\_cpu}, the only service at that distance in this topology), for nine targets total.

\textbf{The result is not what a simple ``signal dilutes with distance'' hypothesis predicts.} Strict accuracy does fall monotonically with hop-distance -- $25.0\%$ (hop 1, $n{=}40$) $\to 2.5\%$ (hop 2, $n{=}40$) $\to 0.0\%$ (hop 3, $n{=}10$), Spearman $\rho=-1.0$ -- consistent with the direct Tier-1 (workload-only) channel genuinely weakening at range: mean $|{\text{Tier-1 contribution}}|$ falls from $0.035$ to $0.009$ over the same range while mean $|{\text{backpressure contribution}}|$ \emph{grows}, from $0.082$ to $0.169$. But once that redirected signal is correctly counted as upstream (the corrected classifier of Section~\ref{sec:tier-v2}), \textbf{upstream accuracy does not decay with distance -- it recovers}: $40.0\%$ (hop 1) $\to 92.5\%$ (hop 2) $\to 90.0\%$ (hop 3). The weakest point in the whole hop range is the closest one. Per-target results at hop 1 are highly variable rather than uniformly weak (\texttt{ts-order-service\_cpu}: $0\%/20\%$ strict/upstream; \texttt{ts-seat-service\_cpu}: $10\%/70\%$; \texttt{ts-travel-service\_cpu}: $10\%/10\%$; \texttt{ts-user-service\_cpu}: $80\%/60\%$), so we treat ``hop-distance'' itself as at most a partial explanation and examine two specific hop-1 nodes -- \texttt{ts-travel-service\_cpu} (worst performer at any hop) and \texttt{ts-user-service\_cpu} (best) -- directly in the next two subsections. (The hop-2/hop-3 samples are thin -- 4 and 1 target respectively -- so we report this recovery as suggestive, not as a settled scaling law.)

\subsection{Per-Node Mechanism Inspection}
\label{sec:mechanism}
For each of the four hop-1 targets, we inspected the fitted mechanism directly: its NNLS (non-negative least squares) coefficient on each parent, that parent's own training-period standard deviation (an \emph{effective scale} $= |\text{coef}| \times \sigma_{\text{parent}}$, a rough proxy for how much a parent's typical variation moves the target), and the mechanism's in-sample $R^2$. This requires matching each fitted coefficient to the correct parent name; DoWhy fits every mechanism against \texttt{sorted(predecessors)}, \emph{not} the causal graph's edge-insertion order -- we discovered this the hard way (an initial pass using insertion order produced an apparently pathological $R^2=-3.67$ for \texttt{ts-travel-service\_cpu} that vanished, becoming a normal $R^2=0.436$, once the column order was corrected to match DoWhy's own convention). We report this correction rather than silently fix it, since a spurious negative-$R^2$ finding would otherwise have been a false lead.

\textbf{With correct column alignment, one pattern is uniform across all four targets, and it is not the one that separates them}: Tier-1's share of total effective scale is small everywhere -- $2.1\%$ (\texttt{ts-order}), $1.5\%$ (\texttt{ts-seat}), $9.0\%$ (\texttt{ts-travel}), $10.8\%$ (\texttt{ts-user}) -- so a target's own fitted mechanism structurally starves the direct workload channel of credit regardless of whether that target succeeds or fails at the intervention-based test above. Static coefficient share therefore cannot, by itself, explain why \texttt{ts-user} succeeds ($80\%$ strict) while \texttt{ts-travel} fails ($10\%$): \texttt{ts-travel} actually has a \emph{larger} Tier-1 share ($9.0\%$) than \texttt{ts-seat} ($1.5\%$, which nonetheless reaches $70\%$ upstream accuracy). Fit quality alone is similarly inconclusive ($R^2 = 0.309, 0.598, 0.436, 0.150$ for order/seat/travel/user respectively -- \texttt{ts-user} has the \emph{worst} fit of the four, yet the best attribution accuracy). The static, in-sample view of the mechanism is necessary context but not sufficient to predict which specific nodes will fail; Section~\ref{sec:propagation} looks one layer upstream of it.

\subsection{True Propagated Signal Magnitude}
\label{sec:propagation}
Rather than a target's own fitted coefficients, we measured directly how far the real $do(\text{ts-preserve-service\_workload}=2.5\times)$ intervention actually moves each target's own \texttt{\_workload} node -- via \texttt{gcm.interventional\_samples}, expressed as a \emph{sigma-shift} (the intervened mean minus the training baseline mean, in units of that node's own training standard deviation) -- rather than inferred from static coefficients. All four targets sit at exactly one Tier-1-only hop from the injection node, yet the true signal reaching them differs by close to a factor of two: \texttt{ts-user-service\_workload} moves $1.201\sigma$ ($+77.1\%$ relative to baseline), against $0.688\sigma$ (\texttt{ts-order}), $0.679\sigma$ (\texttt{ts-travel}), and $0.600\sigma$ (\texttt{ts-seat}).

This aligns with, but does not fully resolve, the attribution outcome: \texttt{ts-user} receives by far the strongest true signal \emph{and} has the best strict accuracy ($80\%$), consistent with a stronger true cause being easier to correctly credit even against a small nominal coefficient share. \texttt{ts-seat} receives the \emph{weakest} true signal ($0.600\sigma$) yet reaches the second-best upstream accuracy ($70\%$) -- explained by its own backpressure parents (\texttt{ts-travel-service\_cpu}, \texttt{ts-travel2-service\_cpu}) themselves being downstream of the same intervention, so credit that would strictly belong to a two-hop-removed Tier-1 signal still lands correctly in the ``upstream'' bucket via the backpressure channel. \texttt{ts-travel-service\_cpu} is the one node with no such rescue: its own true signal is unexceptional ($0.679\sigma$, similar to \texttt{ts-order}'s), and unlike \texttt{ts-seat} it has no dominant backpressure parent that is \emph{itself} clearly downstream of the injected intervention -- combined with a target of intrinsically high residual variance (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}'s $\sigma_{\text{target}}=3.40$, the largest of the four), it is left with neither a strong direct signal nor an indirect rescue path. \textbf{We do not claim a single clean law}: attribution outcome at one hop is better explained as the joint result of (i) true signal strength, (ii) whether nominally-competing backpressure parents happen to be co-downstream of the same true cause, and (iii) the target's own residual noise floor, than by any one of these alone.

\subsection{Comparison Against a Non-Causal Baseline (BARO)}
\label{sec:baro}
The diagnostics above establish \emph{that} and roughly \emph{why} Tier-1 attribution is unreliable at one hop -- but not whether this is a defect specific to interventional Shapley, or a property of the underlying data/topology that would defeat any root-cause ranking method. We compared against BARO \cite{nguyen2024baro}, a non-causal statistical RCA baseline from RCAEval's own reproducible baseline suite: for each candidate column, fit a \texttt{RobustScaler} (median/IQR) on its normal/baseline distribution, transform the anomalous observations through it, and rank columns by maximum absolute robust z-score. We reused BARO's published ranking rule verbatim (RCAEval's \texttt{e2e/baro.py}), skipping only its dataset-specific raw-CSV preprocessing step, on the two most diagnostic targets: \texttt{ts-travel-service\_cpu} (worst performer for Shapley) and \texttt{ts-user-service\_cpu} (best). To keep the comparison apples-to-apples, BARO's candidate set was restricted to exactly the same causal neighborhood \texttt{gcm.attribute\_anomalies} is confined to (the target plus its causal ancestors -- 11 and 7 columns respectively), not RCAEval's usual whole-system scan (an initial, uncorrected attempt at this comparison scanned all 112 columns and produced a near-vacuous ``top-1 is not the target'' pass rate of 100\% on both nodes -- trivially likely by chance alone with 112 candidates, and revealed on inspection that BARO's actual top-1 pick was neither the target nor the true injected node in either case; we report this second bug too rather than the misleading number it produced).

\textbf{Neither method dominates.} On \texttt{ts-travel-service\_cpu}, BARO's ancestor-outranks-target rate is $50.0\%$ against Shapley's $0$--$10\%$ -- BARO does better on the exact node where Shapley fails worst. On \texttt{ts-user-service\_cpu}, BARO's rate is $40.0\%$ against Shapley's $60$--$80\%$ -- Shapley does better on the node where it succeeds. Both methods are trivially correct on the tier2\_driven (local-fault) sanity check ($100\%$ each, both nodes). The true injected node itself never outranks the target in BARO's ranking on either node ($0\%$ of repeats) -- when BARO's best ancestor does win, it is a different intermediate node, not the ultimate root cause, which is itself consistent with the point Section~\ref{sec:background} makes about associational methods conflating ``downstream of many small upstream pushes'' with ``the true origin.'' \textbf{We read this as evidence that correctly attributing a network-propagated cause at this topology scale is a genuinely hard task for causal and non-causal methods alike}, not a defect unique to interventional Shapley -- while noting the comparison covers only 2 of this section's 10 tier-decomposition targets and should not be over-generalized without extending it further.

\subsection{BARO on Real Fault-Injection Data}
\label{sec:baro-real}
Section~\ref{sec:baro}'s comparison, like every other check in this paper, uses synthetic ground-truth-by-construction scenarios (a real or simulated $do(x)$/perturbation on top of the fitted DAG) rather than an actually-occurred incident, and restricts BARO's candidate set to the target's own causal ancestors to keep the comparison matched to what \texttt{gcm.attribute\_anomalies} sees. We additionally ran BARO in its \emph{native} setting -- real, timestamped telemetry with a real injection timestamp, ranking over all $\sim$14 services with no causal-graph restriction -- on RCAEval's own raw Sock Shop corpus (\texttt{data/raw/RE2-SS}): 30 fault types (5 services $\times$ 6 fault categories: CPU, memory, disk, network delay, packet loss, socket exhaustion) $\times$ 3 repeats, the identical 90-scenario corpus the companion systems paper uses for its own RQ1/RQ2 point-forecast accuracy. This is a different task (service-level root-cause localization from a real incident, not attribution of a hypothetical $do(x)$ outcome) and is not directly comparable in accuracy terms to Section~\ref{sec:baro}'s figures -- we report it as an independent data point on how hard this benchmark's root-cause task is for an established method, not as a third arm of that comparison.

\textbf{BARO's own accuracy on real incidents in this benchmark is modest and highly fault-type-dependent}: $26.7\%$ Top-1 and $51.1\%$ Top-3 service-level accuracy overall ($n=90$), against a $\sim$$7\%$ chance baseline over $\sim$14 candidate services. Breaking down by fault type reveals a sharp split: memory faults are comparatively easy ($60.0\%$ Top-1, $93.3\%$ Top-3) and disk faults moderately so ($40.0\%$/$73.3\%$), but \textbf{CPU, network-delay, and packet-loss faults are uniformly hard} -- all three tied at $13.3\%$ Top-1, $26.7\%$ Top-3, the worst of the six categories. \textbf{This is directly relevant to the companion systems paper's own finding} that CPU is the one metric resisting accurate forecasting while memory forecasts cleanly on both testbeds (its ``Why CPU Resists Forecasting'' discussion, attributed there to background OS/GC/health-check noise no request-count metric observes): the same asymmetry -- memory well-behaved, CPU resistant -- shows up here independently, in a real established RCA method's ability to localize the fault's origin at all, not merely in a regression's ability to forecast CPU's future value. We read this as corroborating evidence that CPU's difficulty in this benchmark is a property of the signal itself, observed across two unrelated tasks (forecasting and localization) and two unrelated methods (this paper's monotonicity-constrained SCM and an off-the-shelf statistical RCA baseline), not an artifact specific to either.

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{[TODO --- STUB]:} Draft discussion. Key points to cover:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Evaluation tier (Doshi-Velez \& Kim, 2017).} All experiments here are \emph{functionally-grounded}: faithfulness is checked against a synthetic, self-constructed ground truth, not against a human's judgment of whether the explanation is useful or against an operator's actual scale/no-scale decision. Neither human-grounded nor application-grounded evaluation has been attempted. State this as the paper's primary limitation, not an afterthought.
    \item \textbf{Confound in Section~\ref{sec:tier}'s own design.} In the Tier-1-driven scenario, the target's resource value is left to the fitted mechanism's own prediction rather than held to a known-clean value -- so residual model misfit (not a real local anomaly) could itself register as spurious Tier-2 contribution, potentially explaining part of why Tier-1 accuracy is $<100\%$ even on Sock Shop. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} partially addresses this (in-sample $R^2$ per target is reported alongside coefficient share) but does not fully separate the two effects.
    \item \textbf{Naive-baseline comparison: done, but narrow.} Section~\ref{sec:baro} compares against BARO on 2 of the 10 tier-decomposition targets and finds neither method dominates -- suggestive that the one-hop weakness is a task property, not a Shapley-specific defect, but a comparison on only 2 nodes should not be read as a settled result. Section~\ref{sec:baro-real} corroborates this from a different angle -- BARO's own accuracy on real incidents in the same benchmark is fault-type-dependent and specifically weak on CPU -- but on a different task (real-incident localization) that cannot substitute for extending Section~\ref{sec:baro}'s matched comparison to the full target set. A second non-causal baseline (e.g.\ RCAEval's \texttt{CausalRCA} or \texttt{RCD}, which do use a causal graph but not Shapley values) remains untried.
    \item \textbf{Scale sensitivity is real but not a simple monotonic decay.} Tier-2 (local) attribution is scale-invariant (perfect on both testbeds). Tier-1 (propagation) attribution is markedly worse on Train Ticket than Sock Shop in aggregate, but Section~\ref{sec:hop-distance} shows this is not simply ``bigger graph, weaker signal'': accuracy is worst at the \emph{shortest} distance tested (one hop) and recovers at two-to-three hops once backpressure-mediated credit is counted correctly. The honest summary is that scale changes \emph{which} nodes are hard, not that it uniformly degrades attribution with distance.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Two testbeds only}, of one architectural style (RCAEval's fault-injected microservice benchmarks); generalization to other topologies or fault-injection regimes is untested.
    \item \textbf{Perturbation magnitude in Section~\ref{sec:tier} is large by design} ($+5\sigma$), chosen to give the mechanism its best chance; smaller, more realistic local anomalies have not been tested and may fare worse.
    \item \textbf{The mechanism validated here is not the one deployed}: the companion systems paper's production capacity-forecasting agent uses a simpler fixed-threshold heuristic for its own bottleneck ranking, not this attribution mechanism -- wiring them together and re-validating end-to-end remains open.
    \item \textbf{Thin samples at the topology extremes.} Section~\ref{sec:hop-distance}'s hop-3 group has exactly one target (\texttt{ts-price-service\_cpu}); its $90.0\%$ accuracy should be read as one data point, not a validated rate at that distance.
    \item \textbf{Two independent measurement/analysis bugs were found and corrected mid-study} (backpressure-edge misclassification, Section~\ref{sec:tier-v2}; a parent-column ordering error in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}, and a near-vacuous baseline criterion in Section~\ref{sec:baro}). We disclose all three rather than only the final numbers, but their discovery this late raises the possibility that further, undiscovered measurement issues remain in the diagnostics reported here.
    \item \textbf{The Sock Shop and Precision@17 numbers shifted between recordings} (Sections~\ref{sec:topology}, \ref{sec:tier}) for reasons not fully disentangled -- most likely the backpressure-edge DAG change, possibly compounded by ordinary Monte Carlo variance across independent 10-repeat runs. A fixed random seed and a direct before/after re-run on an unmodified DAG would resolve this and is not yet done.
\end{itemize}
